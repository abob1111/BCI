Choices

As Brain-Computer Interface (BCI) technology rapidly advances from theoretical research into reality, society faces critical choices regarding access, regulation and ethical boundaries. Autonomy is a broad concept, and it has implications for other key ethical concepts such as responsibility, informed consent and privacy. Determining how BCIs are deployed, who is allowed to use them, and where legal limits are drawn will define the future of human-computer interaction.

Public Accessibility

People must choose whether BCIs should be available to the public or restricted to licensed operators, and whether the benefits of BCIs are worth the potential risks, such as privacy concerns, surgery or reliance on technology. Non-invasive devices can be used for entertainment or for communication. In high-stakes environments, such as piloting commercial aircraft or operating surgical robots via BCI, specialists would likely need a certified licence to ensure the user's workload, reaction speed and mental stability meet the highest safety standards. Allowing everyone to use BCIs freely would increase access, but restricting high-risk uses to trained and licensed specialists could protect public safety. We believe a balanced approach, allowing non-invasive BCIs for public use while requiring strict approval for invasive implants and risky applications, would help protect users without limiting access for everyone.

Ethics and Consent

BCIs raise many ethical questions around informed consent. Farisco et al. (2015) note that informed consent must respect disclosure, where the patient has and understands all the information needed; capacity, the patient's ability to understand that information and make a reasonable decision; and voluntariness, a decision made without coercion or undue influence. Many BCI users are non-communicative patients, such as those in a locked-in state, and therefore have significantly impaired capacity to consent. Steps should be taken to ensure that voluntariness is not overridden by desperation, leading to inappropriate consent.

Weaponisation and Clinical Purposes

Brain-to-brain interfaces offer many advantages in hazardous civilian tasks such as sea mining or space exploration, but the same mechanisms could be used as weapons, for drone piloting or military command systems. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA, 2021) treats implanted BCIs as high-risk medical devices intended for patients with paralysis or amputation, focused on restoring functions such as speech or motor control. However, private neurotechnology firms lean towards brain augmentation, so society must decide whether healthy people should be permitted to undergo invasive implantation for superior memory, speed or direct digital control. This could divide society if cognitive enhancement is accessible only to high-income populations.
