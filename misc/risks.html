Risks

Brain computer interfaces give people the opportunity to rehabilitate and reintegrate into society, communicate with computers, and enable human-computer interaction. However, the ability to interpret brain activity also creates risks. These risks extend beyond the individual and affect organizations as well as society as a whole. Research says that BCI risks are not fully understood, making assessments crucial before technology becomes more widely used and adopted (King et al., 2024).

Physical and Psychological harm

A major risk of BCI is physical and psychological harm, especially with invasive BCIs. Implantable BCIs must be placed within or around the brain which raises safety concerns around the procedure and long-term use and effects of the device. Klein and Ojemann (2016) identify the various safety concerns alongside potential cognitive and communicative impairments as critical risks that need to be considered when obtaining informed consent. This is important because BCI technology is used directly with the brain, which means failures may have more serious consequences than failures in conventional computer interfaces. These risks are not limited to medical complications as they can occur throughout the entire system lifecycle and may affect individuals, organizations and society. However, the level of risk depends on the type of BCI being used, as implanted devices may create different risks than non-invasive devices.

Privacy and Security

Privacy and security are another significant risk. BCIs process highly personal information and are capable of interpreting a user's intentions or mental state. Unauthorized access, inappropriate use of brain data or cyber-attacks could have serious consequences for the user. Brocal (2023) notes that cybersecurity is an important area of risk, especially since BCI becomes connected to external technology. Klein and Ojemann (2016) also identify privacy and security as concerns in implantable BCI research. Unlike ordinary personal data, compromised BCI data could potentially reveal information directly related to the user's brain activity, making strong privacy and security controls essential.

Broader risks

Broader risks include the regulation of BCI and human factors. King et al. (2023) identified 18 risk themes and found poor regulation and lack of training of BCI stakeholders to be one of the most concerning risks. It was also found that only one study had used a formal risk assessment method which demonstrated how the knowledge for BCI risk was limited (King et al., 2024). Overall, the evidence suggests that BCI development shouldn’t focus only on technological capability. Regulation, training, cybersecurity, informed consent, and ongoing risk assessment are important to make sure that the benefits of BCI outweigh the risks. While physical risks from brain implants have been identified in research, risks such as hacking and incorrect system decisions still require more research to understand how likely they are.
