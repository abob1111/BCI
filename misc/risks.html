Risks

Brain computer interfaces give people the opportunity to rehabilitate and reintegrate into society, communicate with computers, and enable human-computer interaction. However, the ability to interpret brain activity also creates risks. These risks extend beyond the individual and affect organizations as well as society as a whole. Research says that BCI risks are not fully understood, making assessments crucial before technology becomes more widely used and adopted (King, Read, & Salmon, 2024).

Physical and Psychological harm

A major risk of BCI is physical and psychological harm, especially with invasive BCIs. Implantable BCIs require to be placed within or around the brain which raises safety concerns around the procedure and learn-term use and effects of the device. Klein and Ojemann (2016) identify the various safety concerns alongside potential cognitive and communicative impairments as critical risks that need to be considered when obtaining informed consent. This is important because BCI technology is used directly with the brain, which means failures may have more serious consequences than failures in conventual computer interfaces. These risks are not limited to medical complications for they can occur throughout the entire system lifecycle and may affect individuals, organizations and society.

Privacy and Security

Privacy and security are another significant risk. BCIs process highly personal information and are capable of interpreting a user's intentions or mental state. Unauthorized access, inappropriate use of brain data or cyber-attacks could have serious consequences for the user. Brocal (2023) notes that cybersecurity is an important area of risk, especially since BCI becomes connected to external technology. Klein and Ojemann (2016) also identify privacy and security as concerns in implantable BCI research. Unlike ordinary personal data, compromised BCI data could potentially reveal information directly related to the user's brain activity, making strong privacy and security controls essential.

Autonomy, Identity, and Incorrect System Decisions

There are also risks related to autonomy, identity, and incorrect system decisions. BCIs my incorrectly interpret neural signals and this may result in an unintended command. This could become dangerous if the device controls an external device, such as a vehicle or robotic device. Brocal (2023) says cyberattacks and safety related BCIs are areas in need of further research. Klien and Ojemann (2016) also raise concerns and potential disruptions around autonomy. These issues show that BCI risks are not only technical for they can affect the user and how they understand and their ability to control themselves.

Broader risks

Broader risks include the regulation of BCI and human factors. King et al (2023) identified 18 risk themes and found poor regulation and lack of training of BCI stakeholders to be one of the most concerning risks. It was also found that only one study had used a formal risk assessment method which demonstrated how the knowledge for BCI risk was limited (King et al, 2024). Overall, the evidence suggests that BCI development shouldn’t focus on technological capability. Regulation, training, cybersecurity, informed consent, and ongoing risk assessment are important to make sure that the benefits of BCI outweigh the risks.
